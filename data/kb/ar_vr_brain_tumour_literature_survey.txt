Abstract Background-Brain tumours are spatially heterogeneous lesions whose enhancing tissue, infiltrative margin, necrosis, oedema and relationship to eloquent anatomy are difficult to infer from isolated two -dimensional slices. Objective -This survey evaluates how augmented and virtual r eality, multimodal MRI segmentation, radiomics, machine learning, mathematical growth models and treatment -response simulation can be combined into an interactive, scientifically defensible educational system. Search approach-A PRISMA-inspired search was p erformed on 19 July 2026 using Consensus and Scite alongside PubMed/DOI and publisher metadata checks. Searches covered 2000 -2026, prioritised 2015 -2026, and selected exactly 30 unique peer - reviewed papers: six AR/VR, six segmentation/3D reconstruction, fi ve radiomics, five prediction, four growth - modelling, and four treatment -planning or patient -specific simulation papers. Major findings -Automatic multimodal MRI segmentation and 3D visualization are mature enough for a student prototype; nnU -Net-type pipelines provide a practical baseline, while PyRadiomics supports transparent feature extraction. AR can improve spatial understanding and workflow, but registration, occlusion, depth perception and human factors remain limiting. Prognostic classifiers, future -mask predictors and mechanistic growth simulators a ddress different endpoints and cannot be substituted for one another. Research gaps-Evidence is constrained by small retrospective cohorts, scanner domain shift, weak external validation, scarce longitudinal treatment data, limited uncertainty visualizatio n and few integrated AR -plus-growth systems. Conclusion -A defensible prototype should combine validated segmentation, mesh-based mobile AR and clearly labelled educational scenarios. Any progression output must be probabilistic or mechanistic, visibly unce rtain, clinician -supervised and explicitly non-diagnostic. Clinical forecasting and treatment prescription remain outside the evidence supported by this corpus. Keywords augmented reality; virtual reality; glioma; glioblastoma; multimodal MRI; tumour segmentation; radiomics; machine learning; reaction-diffusion model; treatment response; digital twin; uncertainty visualization. 1. Introduction Brain tumours comprise biologically diverse primary and metastatic neoplasms arising within or affecting the central nervous system. Gliomas are especially relevant to the proposed application because their imaging phenotype often contains enhancing tumour , non -enhancing or necrotic core, and surrounding FLAIR abnormality that may reflect oedema and infiltrative disease. Location matters as much as volume: a small lesion near motor, language, visual or memory networks may be functionally more consequential than a larger lesion in less eloquent tissue. Shape is irregular, infiltration may extend beyond visible enhancement, and surgery or treatment can alter anatomy. These properties make a brain tumour a dynamic spatial process rather than a simple spherical object [8], [15], [23]-[26]. MRI is the principal soft-tissue imaging source for glioma visualization. T1-weighted MRI provides anatomy; postcontrast T1 highlights blood -brain-barrier disruption and enhancement; T2 and FLAIR emphasize water -rich abnormal tissue. CT contributes bone, calcification and acute haemorrhage information, while PET may contribute metabolism or hypoxia, as in patient-specific radiation modelling [25]. Multimodal fusion is therefore scientifically attractive but operationally difficult: images must be de -identified, co-registered, resampled, biascorrected and normalized before labels or features can be compared [8], [12], [16]. Ordinary two-dimensional viewing requires the observer to integrate many slices and mentally transform image coordinates into three -dimensional anatomy. Mixed -reality training was developed specifically to reduce this spatial and cognitive burden [1]. AR overlays virtual objects on the real environment; VR replaces the environment with an immersive one; mixed reality supports spatially anchored interaction. Their value is strongest when a segmented lesion, vessels and functional anatomy can be explored from arbitrary viewpoints. Their limitations are equally concrete: misregistration, occlusion, limited field of view, depth ambiguity, display discomfort, attention capture and motion sickness can turn an apparently compelling visualization into a misleading one [2], [4], [5].

Progression simulation is more difficult than visualization because "future tumour " has several meanings. A classifier can estimate the probability of recurrence; a survival model estimates time-to-event risk; a longitudinal network can forecast a future mask; a reaction -diffusion equation can simulate cell -density evolution; and a generative model can synthesize a plausible image. These outputs differ in target, evidence and validation. A treatment module adds further causal difficulty because surgery, radiation and chemotherapy responses depend on dose, extent of resection, molecular status, pharmacology and patient condition [19], [21], [22], [25]-[27]. The proposed application is intended primarily for education, visualization, research support and treatment explanation. It is not an autonomous diagnostic system and must not prescribe treatment. Simulated trajectories should be labelled as scenarios, not forecasts of certainty; interfaces should show assumptions, confidence or parameter ranges, and the source of each output. For a student project, scientific credibility comes from restricting claims to what has been validated, retaining human oversight, a nd separating an educational animation from a patient-specific research prediction. 2. Literature-Search Methodology A reproducible, PRISMA-inspired search was completed on 19 July 2026. Consensus was used to discover peer - reviewed candidates and retrieve bibliographic records; Scite was used to cross -check metadata, available fulltext excerpts, citation context and edi torial notices. PubMed/PMC, IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect, Nature Portfolio, Wiley, Oxford Academic, Frontiers, MDPI, ACM-indexed proceedings, MICCAI proceedings and the International Journal of Computer Assisted Radiology and Surgery were treat ed as eligible sources. DOI landing pages and stable publisher records were used for final traceability. This report is a structured literature survey, not a registered systematic review. The date range was 2000 -2026, with priority given to 2015 -2026. Older papers were admitted only when foundational: the U -Net architecture, core radiomics methods and proliferation -invasion modelling. Search strings included: ("augmented reality" AND "brain tumour"); ("virtual reality" AND "neurosurgery"); ("mixed reality" AND "brain tumour visualization"); ("brain MRI" AND "3D reconstruction"); ("glioma segmentation" AND "deep learning"); ("BraTS" AND "tumour segmentation"); ("brain tumour radiomics"); ("glioblast oma" AND "radiomics" AND "survival"); ("tumour progression prediction" AND "MRI"); ("glioma recurrence prediction"); ("treatment response prediction" AND "brain tumour"); ("reaction diffusion model" AND "glioma"); ("patient - specific tumour simulation"); ("digital twin" AND "oncology"); ("AR surgical navigation" AND "neurosurgery"); and ("radiotherapy planning" AND "brain tumour"). Inclusion criteria were: peer -reviewed journal or full conference paper; direct relevance to at least one architecture component; traceable title, authorship, year, venue and DOI or stable link; original empirical, computational, data -descriptor or systema tic-review evidence; and sufficient methodological information for critical interpretation. Exclusion criteria were: blogs, news, vendor claims, dissertations, student reports, unpublished presentations, duplicate records, preprints without a peer -reviewed version, papers unrelated to intracranial imaging or transferable methodology, and studies whose bibliographic identity could not be verified. Reviews were retained where they synthesized device or modelling evidence, but were labelled as reviews rather than primary clinical validation. 2.1 PRISMA-Inspired Selection Summary Identification yielded a broad candidate pool from the connector searches and the supplied PDF. Duplicate titles and DOI variants were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to visualization, segmentation, radiomics, prediction, spatial growth or treatment simulation. Full metadata were then assessed for peer-review status and traceability. The supplied seven -page PDF contributed 20 candidate records but included arXiv challenge papers and did not satisfy the exact-30 rule. After replacing non-peer-reviewed or insufficiently relevant records and balancing categories, 30 unique papers were included. Common exclusion reasons were preprint - only status, non-brain applications without essential methodological transfer, absence of a stable bibliographic record, purely commercial description, and claims unsupported by a patient, dataset, simulation or user evaluation. Evidence strength was rated informally. "Strong" indicates a large or multi -centre dataset, systematic review, established benchmark/data resource, external validation, or high reproducibility. Thematic Literature Review 4.1 AR, VR, and Mixed Reality in Medical Visualization Across the six visualization papers, the most defensible benefit is improved access to spatial information. Abhari et al. measured training and planning performance rather than assuming that immersion itself was beneficial [1]. Meola et al. found that neur osurgical AR implementations differed in real -data source, tracking, registration, processing, display and perception location [2]. This taxonomy remains useful: a mobile AR educational model, an optical see-through HMD and a microscope head-up display have different error chains and should not share one undifferentiated accuracy claim. Clinical evidence is strongest where the system is evaluated during real workflow. Roethe et al. prospectively randomized 55 intracranial lesions and found that integrated AR could reduce pointer -based navigation effort, yet virtual content sometimes block ed important anatomy and depth/accuracy ratings remained inferior to conventional neuronavigation [4]. Ivan et al. showed feasible HoloLens incision planning in 11 cases, but a pilot cannot establish outcome benefit [3]. Birlo et al.'s systematic review similarly concluded that much of the OST - HMD literature remained phantom, setup or feasibility work [5]. HoloBrain is particularly relevant because smartphone AR reduces cost and avoids dependence on a headset [6]. A mobile pipeline can use ARKit/ARCore through Unity AR Foundation, but the selected evidence supports the concept of mobile AR more directly than any specific engine choice. Unity is attractive for mesh import, touch gestures and cross-platform deployment; 3D Slicer, VTK and Blender are suita ble for segmentation inspection, surface extraction and mesh repair. These are proposed engineering choices, not claims that one selected study validated every tool. Marker-based registration is simple and reproducible for a classroom tabletop: a printed fiducial provides a known coordinate frame. Markerless plane tracking is more natural but accumulates drift and does not provide anatomical registration. Patient -registered surgical AR requires a separate tracking and registration problem, including fiducials or surface matching and compensation for brain shift. Therefore the student prototype should render a free-standing educational model, not claim surgical navigation. User studies should measure spatial - learning gain, task completion, System Usability Scale, workload and discomfort. Motion sickness is more prominent in VR, while HMD AR adds eyestrain, field-of-view and focal-depth concerns [4], [5]. 4.2 Brain MRI Segmentation and 3D Reconstruction Segmentation is the most technically mature component. The desired labels are commonly whole tumour, tumour core and enhancing tumour, although label definitions vary by BraTS edition and postoperative task. U - Net introduced an encoder-decoder with skip connections that preserves localization while learning contextual features [7]. DeepMedic added multiscale 3D context [9], Myronenko coupled segmentation with autoencoder regularization [10], and nnU-Net automated configuration of preprocessing, patch size, architecture and training strategy [11]. Ranjbarzadeh et al. added attention in a multimodal brain-tumour pipeline [12]. The MRI pipeline should include DICOM-to-NIfTI conversion where needed, de-identification, orientation checks, rigid or affine co -registration, common resampling, skull stripping when appropriate, N4 -type bias -field correction and intensity normalization. Preprocessing must be fitted within each training fold to prevent leakage. Missing modalities require either exclusion, modality-robust training or explicit synthesis validation; silently filling missing sequences is unsafe. Bakas et al. linked expert labels and radiomic features to TCGA glioma imaging, creating a traceable bridge between segmentation, imaging phenotypes and molecular data [8]. However, challenge performance does not guarantee clinical generalization. Multi -institutional variation can help robustness, but postoperative cavities, blood products, treatment effect and acquisition differences create new domains. Dice is useful for overlap, while Hausdorff distance exposes boundary outliers; both should be reported for each tumour region. For visualization, a predicted voxel mask should be inspected, cleaned only with documented morphology, converted to an isosurface (for example marching cubes), smoothed conservatively, decimated, checked for topology and exported as glTF/GLB or FBX. The t ransform from voxel indices to physical coordinates must be preserved. Brain regions, tumour subregions and vessels should be separate meshes with consistent coordinate

Volume rendering may retain uncertainty better than a hard surface; the app can offer both. 4.3 Radiomics and Imaging Biomarkers Radiomics converts an image and region of interest into numerical descriptors. Shape features quantify volume, surface area, sphericity and elongation; first -order features summarize intensity distributions; texture matrices include GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, NGT DM and GLDM; filtered images can yield wavelet features. Aerts et al. established the broader concept of imaging phenotypes and emphasized stability [13]. PyRadiomics subsequently supplied a transparent, open implementation with documented feature definitions [14]. Glioblastoma studies demonstrate both promise and fragility. Kickingereder et al. developed and validated an MRI radiomic survival signature beyond established clinical/radiologic models [15]. He et al. extracted 107 features per sequence and used LASSO-based models to predict IDH, MGMT and TERT status in 81 patients [17]. Carré et al. showed that scanner/protocol standardization and intensity normalization materially affect robustness and classification [16]. A valid pipeline segments first, extracts features with fixed settings, removes unstable or redundant variables, performs selection inside nested cross -validation, and evaluates on a held -out site. Harmonization such as ComBat may reduce centre effects but must not use test -centre information during fitting. Radiomics depends on contour quality, voxel size, intensity normalization and discretization. Small samples combined with thousands of features invite overfitting; leakage occurs when feature selection precedes data splitting. External validation, calibration and decision-curve analysis are therefore more informative than a high internal AUC alone. For the proposed system, radiomics should be explanatory: show tumour volume, surface-to-volume ratio and a small validated subset of texture features with plain-language definitions. Biomarker and survival outputs should be optional research demonstrations. A feature dashboard is more defensible than presenting an unvalida ted "tumour aggressiveness score." 4.4 Machine Learning for Tumour Progression and Treatment Response The prediction papers address non -equivalent endpoints. Lao et al. predicted overall survival risk from deep radiomic features [18]. Charlton et al. compared one-year survival models and found that interpretable methods could remain competitive when clinic al variables were informative [20]. Kwiatkowska -Miernik et al. estimated progression-free survival in only 51 recurrent high -grade glioma patients [21]. These are risk/time models; none yields a validated future 3D tumour boundary. Pei et al. did attempt future-segmentation prediction using longitudinal MRI and a reaction-diffusion-derived celldensity feature, but evaluated only nine longitudinal subjects [19]. The study is directly relevant and evidentially limited. Chang et al. cl assified recurrent glioblastoma versus pseudoprogression in a multi -institutional setting [22]; this is treatment -state discrimination, not growth forecasting. Pseudoprogression can mimic true progression after therapy, so a simulator must avoid equating increased enhancement with biological growth. Random forests, support-vector machines and gradient boosting are suitable for tabular radiomics/clinical data; CNNs learn spatial features; recurrent networks or temporal transformers may model repeated scans; survival models handle censoring. Multimodal learning can combine imaging, age, performance status, treatment and molecular variables, but missingness and temporal leakage must be controlled. Explainability should match the model: coefficients or SHAP values for tabular models, saliency with sanity c hecks for imaging, and explicit parameter sensitivity for mechanistic simulation. Calibration and uncertainty are essential. Classification should report AUC, sensitivity, specificity and calibration; survival should report concordance and time -dependent calibration; future masks should report spatial Dice, surface distance and volume e rror at specified horizons. Ensembles, Bayesian approximations or conformal prediction can quantify uncertainty, while out -of-distribution detection should warn when scanner, tumour type or postoperative appearance differs from training data. A visually plausible generated scan is not evidence of biological accuracy.

Swanson et al. formalized differential motility in grey and white matter [23], and Harpold et al. reviewed the evolution and clinical interpretation of proliferation-invasion modelling [24]. Parameters have physical meaning, which aids interpretation, but they are only partially identifiable from sparse MRI thresholds. Patient-specific models estimate proliferation, invasion and sometimes treatment sensitivity from serial scans. Rockne et al. added hypoxia -modulated radiation resistance informed by FMISO -PET [25]. Lipková et al. integrated mechanistic modelling, genomics and radiomics for personalized radiotherapy design [26]. These studies demonstrate hypothesis generation and treatment-design research, not a licensed clinical forecaster. Cellular automata discretize tissue into local states; agent -based models represent individual cells and interactions; biomechanical models add mass effect and tissue deformation. Each increases parameter burden. Boundary conditions at skull, ventricles an d tissue interfaces alter simulated spread; anisotropic diffusion can follow white-matter tracts. Longitudinal calibration is necessary, and validation must compare forecasts made before the future scan-not merely fit all time points retrospectively. For education, a reaction -diffusion model is valuable because users can see how proliferation and invasion change spatial patterns. For research demonstrations it can support sensitivity analysis and hypothesis generation. Individual clinical forecasting remains uncertain because routine MRI does not directly measure cell density, biology changes under treatment, and model -form error is substantial. The interface should therefore display parameter ranges and ensembles rather than one deterministic surface. 4.6 Treatment Simulation and Patient-Specific Modelling Treatment visualization and response prediction must be labelled separately. Hormuth et al. calibrated mechanism-based temozolomide -response models in patient -derived xenografts [27]; this is stronger than a purely hypothetical animation but remains precli nical. Sørensen et al. showed why public preoperative BraTS data cannot automatically be treated as a complete postoperative response cohort [28]. A surgery mode can visualize removal of a selected mask and residual -volume calculation, but without deformation, infiltration and postoperative imaging it is a resection demonstration, not outcome prediction. Radiotherapy simulation can display a prescribed dose field, organs at risk and a modelled cell -kill scenario. Rockne and Lipková provide mechanistic precedents [25], [26], yet real patient planning requires calibrated dose, fractionation, registration, contour review and clinical quality assurance. Chemotherapy scenarios should vary sensitive/resistant parameters rather than claim a certain shrinkage. "No treatment" may show natural -history model ensembles but needs strong warnings. Digital twins imply repeated assimilation of patient observations into a model that represents current state, forecasts alternatives and updates as new data arrive [29]. The selected paper is a perspective, so it supports design principles-not proof that a brain-tumour digital twin is clinically available. SLIMBRAIN demonstrates a different form of patient-specific assistance: real-time hyperspectral classification mapped into an AR scene at 14 frames per second [30]. It supports feasibility of real-time overlay, not longitudinal response prediction. A credible scenario comparator should show actual baseline scan, segmented anatomy, model choice, assumptions, uncertainty band and outcome measures such as volume trajectory. It should distinguish real observations from simulated frames by colour and labelling. Clinician review is mandatory whenever patient data are used. 5. Comparative Analysis Direct numerical ranking across the corpus would be misleading. Segmentation papers differ in BraTS edition, training size, label definition and lesion -wise versus voxel -wise metrics [9] -[12]. Radiomics studies differ in endpoint, feature preprocessing and validation [15]-[17]. Survival AUC, concordance index and progression-free survival error are not interchangeable [18], [20], [21]. AR task time and registration error address usability and geometry rather than biological accuracy [1], [3] -[6]. Accordingly, comparisons below emphasize evidence and task suitability rather than a league table.

A model trained on preoperative adult glioma should not be presented as valid for metastases, meningioma, paediatric tumours or postoperative scans. • Dataset and validation: strongest generalization evidence comes from systematic reviews, multi-institutional resources and external validation [5], [8], [11], [15], [22]. Small pilots and longitudinal cohorts provide feasibility but unstable estimates [3], [19], [21]. Internal cross-validation is not independent clinical validation. • Accuracy and metrics: Dice and Hausdorff are appropriate for masks; AUC, sensitivity and specificity for binary states; C-index for time-to-event ranking; calibration for probability reliability; registration error and task metrics for AR. Computational requirements rise from tabular models to 3D CNNs, temporal models, physics solvers and real-time rendering. nnU-Net training usually needs a capable GPU, but inference and decimated-mesh rendering can be separated so the mobile device remains responsive. • Interpretability and real-time suitability: handcrafted radiomics and reaction-diffusion parameters are more directly interpretable than deep latent features, but neither is automatically correct. Mobile AR is real-time after mesh preparation [6]; hyperspectral intraoperative classification reached 14 fps with specialized hardware [30]. Growth simulation may be precomputed into timeline states. A student prototype should prioritize reliable interaction over running every model on-device. 6. Research Gaps No integrated end -to-end system: AR studies focus on visualization/navigation, whereas progression and treatment papers run offline models; none validates a combined mobile AR, radiomics, growth and scenario platform [3]-[6], [19], [25]-[30]. Longitudinal scarcity: Future-mask work used nine longitudinal subjects, and public BraTS was not designed as a complete postoperative response cohort [19], [28]. Small and single -centre cohorts: Biomarker and PFS studies used 81 and 51 patients, respectively, limiting stable feature selection and calibration [17], [21]. External validation and reproducibility: Many pipelines rely on internal splits; scanner/protocol effects change radiomic features and challenge models may not transfer to routine care [11], [12], [16], [17]. Domain shift: Institution, field strength, sequence parameters, tumour type and treatment state alter input distributions [8], [16], [28]. Uncertainty communication: Most visualization studies display surfaces rather than calibrated spatial uncertainty; deterministic overlays may appear more certain than source segmentation or registration permits [4], [5]. Limited usability evidence: Clinical AR trials are few; occlusion, depth, comfort and workload require broader clinician, student and patient testing [4], [5]. Registration standardization: HMD and mobile systems use differing landmarks, tracking and error definitions, preventing straightforward comparison [2]-[6]. Weak multimodal integration: Radiomics, clinical, molecular and mechanistic variables are usually studied separately; integrated approaches are uncommon and at risk of missing-data bias [17], [20], [26]. Few causal treatment simulations: PFS prediction and recurrence classification do not estimate counterfactual benefit of surgery, radiation or chemotherapy [21], [22], [25]-[27]. Explainability and clinician comparison: Feature importance alone does not establish clinical reasoning; few studies compare calibrated model decisions against clinician judgement across centres [17], [20], [21]. Ethics, privacy and ordinary -device deployment: Patient-linked imaging requires de -identification and governance, while low-cost smartphone evidence remains feasibility-stage [6]. Risk is amplified when simulated growth is communicated to patients as certain.

Proposed Research Framework The architecture should be modular so an error or unsupported claim in one component does not contaminate the rest. The data layer receives anonymized DICOM or NIfTI; validates orientation, spacing and modality; converts when necessary; and stores provenan ce. The imaging layer registers and normalizes sequences, performs segmentation and produces tumour volumes and 3D surfaces. The analytics layer extracts radiomics, joins selected clinical variables and runs either a validated risk model or a mechanistic scenario engine. The presentation layer renders anatomy in AR/VR with a timeline, labels, measuremen ts, uncertainty and a clear "observed versus simulated" state. • MRI acquisition and ingestion: local de-identified NIfTI for the student version; governed DICOM gateway only with hospital approval. • Preprocessing: SimpleITK/NiBabel for orientation, registration, resampling, bias correction and normalization; log transforms and versions. • Segmentation: MONAI/PyTorch implementation or nnU-Net baseline for whole tumour, core and enhancing tumour; retain probability maps. • 3D generation: 3D Slicer/VTK marching cubes, conservative smoothing/decimation, anatomical labels and physical-coordinate validation. • Radiomics: PyRadiomics with fixed binning and resampling; feature selection only inside training folds; optional ComBat fitted without leakage. • Clinical integration: age, diagnosis, molecular markers and treatment data in a minimal schema with missingness indicators and access control. • Progression module: choose one of (a) predefined educational trajectories, (b) calibrated reaction-diffusion ensembles, or (c) research-only longitudinal future-mask prediction. Never merge outputs without labels. • Treatment comparator: surgery as geometric resection demonstration; radiotherapy as dose/response scenario; chemotherapy as parameterized sensitivity scenario; no-treatment as modelled natural-history range. • Uncertainty/XAI: segmentation probability, scenario ensemble, feature importance, calibration plot, out-ofdistribution warning and model card. • AR/VR rendering: Unity plus AR Foundation for ARKit/ARCore; optional Meta Quest/HTC Vive VR. Touch/controller rotation, scale, clipping planes, opacity, region isolation and reset. • Timeline: time-stamped observed scans and visually distinct simulated states; tumour-volume curve with confidence band. • Validation/privacy: expert annotation comparison, longitudinal holdout, site holdout, audit logs, encryption, anonymization and clinician sign-off. 7.1 Minimum Viable Student Prototype Use public preoperative BraTS/TCIA NIfTI, a pretrained or locally trained nnU -Net/MONAI segmentation model, 3D Slicer or VTK mesh conversion, Unity AR Foundation and local on -device or LAN storage. Provide sample cases, anatomical labels, volume measurement, clipping planes and a timeline containing predefined educational growth sc enarios. Allow treatment toggles only as clearly labelled animations based on user - controlled assumptions. Target 30 -60 fps after mesh loading. No patient diagnosis, no automatic treatment recommendation, and no claim of future accuracy. 7.2 Advanced Research Version Add longitudinal MRI, PyRadiomics, clinical/molecular covariates, calibrated survival or recurrence models, reaction-diffusion ensembles, uncertainty maps, prospective forecast locking and comparison with later scans. Containerize preprocessing, track model/data versions and evaluate on a held-out institution. Provide researcher controls for parameter priors and sensitivity analysis, but keep patient-facing language conservative. 7.3 Possible Future Clinical-Grade Version A clinical-grade version would require hospital DICOM integration, validated device calibration, formal software lifecycle controls, cybersecurity, usability engineering, prospective multicentre trials, documented risk management, regulatory classification , post-market monitoring and accountable clinician oversight. Ethical and Clinical Considerations • Privacy: Remove direct DICOM identifiers, inspect burned-in pixels and private tags, use coded subject IDs, minimize retained metadata, encrypt data in transit and at rest, separate identity keys, apply role-based access and record audit events. Public datasets still require compliance with their licences. • Consent and governance: Hospital data require ethics approval or an applicable waiver, data-use agreements and clear consent for education or patient counselling. Secondary use and model training must be disclosed where required. • Bias: Dataset composition, scanner availability, tumour subtype and treatment access influence model performance. Report subgroup results and avoid assuming that BraTS represents every hospital population. • Explainability and uncertainty: Users must see whether a frame is measured, segmented, predicted or simulated. Parameter ranges, probability calibration and model limitations should accompany animations. Synthetic images need watermarks or equivalent provenance to prevent confusion with acquired scans. • Clinical responsibility and regulation: A system that influences diagnosis or treatment may qualify as medicaldevice software and would require jurisdiction-specific regulatory evidence. Clinicians retain responsibility; a classroom prototype must not be used to choose a patient's therapy. • Patient communication: Immersive graphics can appear authoritative and may increase distress. Counselling should use neutral language, avoid deterministic timelines, allow clinicians to hide experimental features and provide a plain-language explanation that scenarios are not medical predictions. 11. Conclusion Current research supports an integrated educational prototype, but the components have unequal maturity. Multimodal MRI segmentation, mesh generation and interactive 3D visualization are the most mature. Radiomics can add quantitative descriptors when prep rocessing and validation are controlled. AR can improve spatial understanding, yet registration, occlusion, depth perception and usability remain active engineering problems. Survival, recurrence and pseudoprogression models answer clinically relevant questions but do not simulate spatial growth. Reaction -diffusion and treatment models provide interpretable research scenarios, not certain patient forecasts. The recommended student direction is therefore a mobile AR bra in/tumour viewer with validated segmentation, transparent volume and radiomic measurements, predefined educational scenarios and an optional parameterized growth demonstration with uncertainty. Clinical deployment would require longitudinal multicentre data, prospective validation, human-factors engineering, cybersecurity, governance and regulatory evidence. 12. Future Scope Future research may develop iterative digital twins that assimilate new scans; multimodal foundation models combining images, reports, genomics and treatment; temporal transformers for longitudinal forecasting; federated learning across hospitals; multicen tre domain-generalization studies; physics -informed neural networks that combine reaction -diffusion constraints with data; uncertainty -aware spatial ensembles; personalized radiotherapy optimization; real -time surgical AR that compensates for brain shift; hap tic tumourresection training; collaborative VR for remote planning; and clinician -in-the-loop systems that record corrections and recalibrate safely. Each direction needs prospective validation and explicit governance before clinical influence [25], [26], [29], [30].
