Interactive Spatial Computing and Biophysical Simulation Framework for Neuro-Oncology A Literature Survey and Technical Implementation Report AR / VR in Neuro-Oncology - Brain Tumour Visualization & Biophysical Simulation Combined Project Report

Abstract Intracranial neoplasms are characterized by intense spatial heterogeneity, irregular margins, necrotic cores, and surrounding infiltrative zones that are difficult to visualize from standard two-dimensional slices. Spatial computing offers a powerful educational and planning interface to reduce cognitive load, yet translating these visualizations into clinically actionable models requires the integration of automated deep-learning segmentation, high-dimensional radiomic profiling, and mathematical biophysical models of tumour growth. This report synthesizes thirty core peer-reviewed publications spanning spatial visualization, image segmentation, quantitative radiomics, predictive machine learning, and mechanistic growth modelling. The analysis reveals that while image segmentation and three-dimensional mesh extraction are highly mature, significant barriers persist in cross-machine protocol harmonization, postoperative cavity modelling, and individual longitudinal validation. To address these limitations, this report details the engineering architecture and deployment of a mobile augmented-reality educational prototype, demonstrating how a 3D reaction-diffusion solver can be integrated with customized treatment scenarios to provide a clear, interactive visualization of cellular progression. Keywords Augmented Reality; Virtual Reality; Glioma; Glioblastoma; Multimodal MRI; Tumour Segmentation; Radiomics; Machine Learning; Reaction-Diffusion Model; Treatment Response; Digital Twin; Uncertainty Visualization. Literature Survey of Thirty Key Papers Each of the thirty reviewed papers is analyzed across seven structured parameters - methodology, focus area, advantage, disadvantage, drawbacks, reported accuracy, and application - to give a consistent, comparable picture of the current state of the art. Paper 1: Training for Planning Tumour Resection Abhari et al., 2015 • Methodology: Evaluated a mixed-reality training platform against standard 2D slice viewing across 36 participants divided into three experience levels. • Focus Area: Spatial training for tumour-resection planning in neurosurgical education. • Advantage: Significantly reduces cognitive load and mental-transformation errors when translating 2D images into 3D understanding. • Disadvantage: Relies on static, simulated training environments rather than live surgical conditions. • Drawbacks: Cannot dynamically compensate for intraoperative variables such as brain shift. • Accuracy: Achieved high spatial-overlap metrics versus 2D displays (p<0.01 for non-clinicians; p<0.05 for clinical completion speed). • Application: Neurosurgical resident and trainee spatial-planning education. Paper 2: Augmented Reality in Neurosurgery Meola et al., 2017 • Methodology: Systematic review mapping AR hardware displays, spatial tracking modalities, and coordinateregistration methods used in prior studies. • Focus Area: Taxonomy of neurosurgical AR system architectures and error sources. • Advantage: Builds a comprehensive taxonomy classifying error chains across mobile, head-mounted, and microscope-embedded overlays. • Disadvantage: Synthesis is purely qualitative, drawn from existing pilot trials rather than new experimental data. • Drawbacks: No standardized clinical guidelines exist to help developers minimize calibration drift.

• Accuracy: Reports wide registration-error variation, from 0.5 mm in optimized setups to over 5.0 mm in uncalibrated environments. • Application: Reference framework for developers designing neurosurgical AR display systems. Paper 3: HMD-Based Incision Planning in Cranial Neurosurgery Ivan et al., 2021 • Methodology: Prospective pilot study using the Microsoft HoloLens 1 for preoperative craniotomy incision planning across 11 cranial cases. • Focus Area: Consumer HMD-based surface planning for craniotomy incisions. • Advantage: Shows that low-cost, consumer-grade optical see-through headsets can project tumour meshes for direct skin marking. • Disadvantage: Restricted to pre-incision surface planning, without deep-tissue pathfinding capability. • Drawbacks: Small pilot cohort limits the statistical power of its clinical conclusions. • Accuracy: Achieved clinical acceptability across all 11 cases, with overlay tracking errors within acceptable surgical tolerances. • Application: Preoperative incision-site marking in cranial neurosurgery. Paper 4: Augmented Reality Visualization in Brain Lesions Roethe et al., 2022 • Methodology: Prospective randomized controlled trial comparing microscope-integrated AR overlays with conventional displays in 55 patients. • Focus Area: Intraoperative navigation efficiency using AR overlays. • Advantage: Significantly reduces manual pointer-based navigation steps, improving workflow continuity. • Disadvantage: Virtual overlays frequently obscure structural detail of deep anatomical pathways. • Drawbacks: Hard-edged overlays fail to accurately represent depth or true tissue boundaries. • Accuracy: Clinicians rated the AR system's depth perception as inferior to conventional displays. • Application: Intraoperative microscope-based tumour navigation. Paper 5: Utility of Optical See-Through Head-Mounted Displays Birlo et al., 2022 • Methodology: Systematic review analyzing optical alignment, field of view, spatial latency, and eye strain across major HMD platforms. • Focus Area: Cross-domain clinical-utility assessment of optical see-through HMDs. • Advantage: Provides critical comparative analysis of optical and ergonomic HMD performance parameters. • Disadvantage: Highly heterogeneous source data prevents a quantitative meta-analysis of registration limits. • Drawbacks: Most published systems remain confined to phantom or laboratory studies without sterile clinical validation. • Accuracy: Identified tracking-drift errors ranging from 1.2 mm to 3.0 mm under sterile laboratory conditions. • Application: Comparative reference for selecting surgical HMD hardware. Paper 6: HoloBrain - Low-Cost Mobile Augmented Reality Guerroudji et al., 2024 • Methodology: Built a low-cost mobile AR pipeline using the Gradient Vector Flow (GVF) snake model for tumour boundary detection. • Focus Area: Mobile, low-barrier interactive 3D brain-model visualization. • Advantage: Segments, constructs, and displays interactive 3D brain models directly on standard mobile devices. • Disadvantage: The GVF snake model requires manual initialization and struggles with image noise. • Drawbacks: Lacks depth-occlusion capability when the model is rendered over real-world geometry. • Accuracy: Maintained stable visual alignment while reaching consumer-device rendering rates of 30 FPS.

• Application: Low-cost educational mobile AR brain visualization. Paper 7: Convolutional Networks for Biomedical Image Segmentation (U-Net) Ronneberger et al., 2015 • Methodology: Introduced a symmetric contracting-expanding convolutional network with skip connections for biomedical image segmentation. • Focus Area: Foundational 2D deep-learning architecture for medical image segmentation. • Advantage: Skip connections preserve fine spatial localization even when trained on small datasets. • Disadvantage: Architecture is strictly two-dimensional, ignoring slice-to-slice 3D spatial consistency. • Drawbacks: Highly vulnerable to performance degradation under scanner or domain shift. • Accuracy: Achieved top-tier benchmark results across several standard segmentation challenges. • Application: Foundational backbone used by most modern tumour-segmentation networks. Paper 8: Advancing Glioma MRI Collections Bakas et al., 2017 • Methodology: Compiled expert-consensus segmentation labels and multi-sequence radiomic features for 461 glioma subjects (BraTS/TCGA). • Focus Area: Public benchmark dataset creation linking imaging phenotypes to genomics. • Advantage: Creates a standardized public database directly linking imaging phenotypes to genomic profiles. • Disadvantage: Dataset is exclusively preoperative, offering limited utility for postoperative recurrence modelling. • Drawbacks: Retrospective curation risks selection bias, limiting clinical generalizability. • Accuracy: Manual consensus labels show high inter-rater consistency, serving as the reference standard for ML challenges. • Application: Standard training and validation dataset for glioma-segmentation research. Paper 9: Efficient Multi-Scale 3D CNN (DeepMedic) Kamnitsas et al., 2017 • Methodology: Formulated a multi-scale 3D CNN with parallel convolutional pathways processing patches at multiple resolutions, refined by CRFs. • Focus Area: Volumetric 3D CNN segmentation with structured post-processing. • Advantage: Preserves true volumetric spatial consistency across voxel slices, minimizing false positives. • Disadvantage: Integrating fully connected Conditional Random Fields is computationally intensive. • Drawbacks: CRF post-processing occasionally over-smooths thin, infiltrative tumour margins. • Accuracy: Demonstrated top-tier benchmark performance with a structural Dice coefficient exceeding 0.85. • Application: 3D volumetric segmentation of tumour sub-regions. Paper 10: 3D MRI Brain Tumor Segmentation Myronenko, 2019 • Methodology: Developed a 3D encoder-decoder network coupled with a parallel variational autoencoder branch for reconstruction regularization. • Focus Area: Regularized volumetric segmentation for limited clinical cohorts. • Advantage: Autoencoder regularization prevents overfitting when training data is limited. • Disadvantage: Dual-decoder training requires massive GPU memory allocation. • Drawbacks: Does not account for post-therapeutic sequence variation or resection-cavity boundaries. • Accuracy: Won first place in BraTS 2018, posting a mean whole-tumour Dice score of 0.90. • Application: High-accuracy preoperative whole-tumour segmentation.

Paper 11: Self-Configuring Method for Biomedical Image Segmentation (nnU-Net) Isensee et al., 2021 • Methodology: Automates spatial preprocessing, batch sizes, learning rates, and architectural rules based on dataset metadata. • Focus Area: Generalizable, self-configuring automated segmentation framework. • Advantage: Removes the need for manual hyperparameter tuning and generalizes across diverse scanner classes. • Disadvantage: Search time required to establish optimal configurations is computationally intensive. • Drawbacks: Bypasses localized architectural customization that could target rare boundary shapes. • Accuracy: Outperformed manually customized architectures across dozens of international segmentation challenges. • Application: De facto standard automated pipeline for medical image segmentation. Paper 12: Brain Tumor Segmentation Based on Deep Learning (PLU-Net) Ranjbarzadeh et al., 2021 • Methodology: Implemented a spatial-attention 3D U-Net variant to emphasize variable tumour boundaries and infiltrative zones. • Focus Area: Attention-enhanced segmentation of infiltrative tumour margins. • Advantage: High sensitivity for delineating highly irregular, infiltrative tumour margins. • Disadvantage: High sensitivity to voxel-intensity variation requires intensive normalization steps. • Drawbacks: Multi-modal registration errors can propagate directly into segmentation failures. • Accuracy: Achieved a state-of-the-art Dice coefficient of 0.91 and a Hausdorff95 distance of 2.5 on BraTS. • Application: Precision delineation of diffuse tumour margins for treatment planning. Paper 13: Decoding Tumour Phenotype Using Quantitative Radiomics Aerts et al., 2014 • Methodology: Established the radiomics framework, extracting 440 high-throughput shape, intensity, and texture features per tumour. • Focus Area: Foundational quantitative-radiomics methodology. • Advantage: Correlates non-invasive texture signatures with tumour gene expression and patient survival. • Disadvantage: Early feature definitions lacked standardization, leading to software-dependent variation. • Drawbacks: Highly dependent on manual region-of-interest annotation. • Accuracy: Successfully stratified survival risk across more than 1,019 lung and head-and-neck cancer patients. • Application: Foundational methodology underlying non-invasive tumour phenotyping. Paper 14: Computational Radiomics System to Decode the Radiographic Phenotype (PyRadiomics) van Griethuysen et al., 2017 • Methodology: Built an open-source Python platform implementing IBSI-standardized shape, intensity, and texture feature extraction. • Focus Area: Standardized, reproducible radiomic feature-extraction software. • Advantage: Open, transparent codebase improves reproducibility of radiomic studies. • Disadvantage: Extracted features remain sensitive to pre-processing choices such as voxel resampling. • Drawbacks: Requires manual multi-center normalization to prevent spurious correlations. • Accuracy: Delivers mathematically validated, standardized feature calculations across radiological benchmarks. • Application: Standard toolkit underlying most modern radiomics research. Paper 15: Radiomic Profiling of Glioblastoma Kickingereder et al., 2016

• Methodology: Extracted 12,190 radiomic features from pre-treatment MRI of 119 glioblastoma patients using supervised principal component analysis. • Focus Area: Radiomic survival-prediction signature development. • Advantage: Multiparametric radiomic signature outperforms established clinical risk metrics. • Disadvantage: Predictive framework is restricted to newly diagnosed glioblastoma cases. • Drawbacks: Retrospective, single-center design is highly vulnerable to local protocol bias. • Accuracy: The 11-feature signature predicted overall survival with a validation C-index of 0.654. • Application: Prognostic risk stratification at initial diagnosis. Paper 16: Standardization of Brain MR Images Carré et al., 2020 • Methodology: Evaluated ComBat harmonization and histogram normalization to stabilize radiomic features across scanner protocols. • Focus Area: Multi-scanner radiomic feature harmonization. • Advantage: Mitigates scanner-dependent variation, improving feature reproducibility across sites. • Disadvantage: Harmonization steps can inadvertently erase genuine micro-heterogeneity signals. • Drawbacks: Does not address real-time scanner integration requirements. • Accuracy: Shows multi-scanner domain shift corrupts up to 70% of raw texture parameters unless corrected. • Application: Essential preprocessing step for multi-center radiomics studies. Paper 17: Multiparametric MR Radiomics in Brain Glioma He et al., 2022 • Methodology: Applied multiparametric MRI radiomics and LASSO modelling to predict IDH, MGMT, and TERT status in 81 glioma patients. • Focus Area: Non-invasive genetic biomarker prediction. • Advantage: Classifies mutational status non-invasively, bypassing surgical biopsy delays. • Disadvantage: Small cohort size increases the risk of feature-selection leakage during validation. • Drawbacks: Requires complete, synchronized multi-parametric scan sets with no missing modalities. • Accuracy: Achieved predictive AUC values greater than 0.80 for non-invasive IDH classification. • Application: Pre-surgical molecular subtyping of gliomas. Paper 18: Deep Learning-Based Radiomics Model in GBM Lao et al., 2017 • Methodology: Trained deep convolutional networks to extract latent imaging features directly from glioblastoma MRI, bypassing handcrafted features. • Focus Area: Deep-learning-based radiomics for survival prediction. • Advantage: Captures complex, non-linear textures that traditional handcrafted features overlook. • Disadvantage: Deep latent feature spaces function as mathematical black boxes, preventing clinical audit. • Drawbacks: High susceptibility to model overfitting on smaller clinical cohorts. • Accuracy: Achieved superior overall-survival prediction compared with handcrafted features on TCGA data. • Application: Automated survival-prediction pipelines from raw imaging. Paper 19: Longitudinal Brain Tumor Segmentation Prediction Pei et al., 2020 • Methodology: Developed a label-fusion network combining longitudinal patient scans with reaction-diffusion modelling to predict future tumour shape. • Focus Area: Spatial forecasting of future tumour growth. • Advantage: Directly forecasts multi-dimensional spatial expansion rather than abstract survival probabilities.

• Disadvantage: Compiling multi-temporal datasets with known treatment markers is extremely difficult. • Drawbacks: Very small sample size (9 patients) limits statistical significance. • Accuracy: Achieved high localized boundary precision within its limited validation cohort. • Application: Early prototype for predictive, patient-specific tumour-growth visualization. Paper 20: Prediction Models for One-Year Brain Tumour Survival Charlton et al., 2023 • Methodology: Compared deep-learning networks against classical machine-learning models for one-year survival prediction using clinical covariates. • Focus Area: Interpretable, clinically auditable survival-prediction modelling. • Advantage: Demonstrates that transparent ML models remain highly competitive when combined with key clinical covariates. • Disadvantage: Dependent on complete, standardized clinical records. • Drawbacks: Does not incorporate spatial coordinates, limiting utility for visual treatment planning. • Accuracy: Achieved competitive classification performance (AUC > 0.78) with full SHAP-based auditability. • Application: Explainable clinical decision support for survival estimation. Paper 21: Estimating Progression-Free Survival Kwiatkowska-Miernik et al., 2024 • Methodology: Evaluated 51 recurrent high-grade glioma patients to predict progression-free survival using clinical metrics and volumetric radiomics. • Focus Area: Recurrence-focused progression-free-survival prediction. • Advantage: Focuses specifically on high-grade glioma recurrence planning. • Disadvantage: Small retrospective cohort size limits validation robustness. • Drawbacks: Treats recurrence as a non-spatial statistical event, ignoring localized cellular kinetics. • Accuracy: Established moderate prognostic predictions by linking baseline tumour volume with patient age. • Application: Post-recurrence prognosis estimation. Paper 22: Prediction of Pseudoprogression versus Progression Jang et al., 2018 • Methodology: Implemented machine-learning classifiers to differentiate true glioblastoma progression from treatment-induced pseudoprogression. • Focus Area: Post-therapy imaging response classification. • Advantage: Helps prevent premature chemotherapy discontinuation by identifying inflammatory contrast enhancement. • Disadvantage: Depends on highly standardized follow-up imaging timelines, which are often inconsistent in practice. • Drawbacks: Strictly classifier-based; cannot model physical tissue spatial evolution. • Accuracy: Demonstrated reliable performance classifying pseudoprogression versus true progression in posttherapy glioblastoma. • Application: Post-treatment surveillance decision support. Paper 23: A Quantitative Model for Differential Motility of Gliomas Swanson et al., 2000 • Methodology: Formulated the foundational biophysical reaction-diffusion model simulating glioma cell migration and proliferation across gray and white matter. • Focus Area: Mechanistic mathematical modelling of tumour cell spread. • Advantage: Model parameters carry direct physical and biological meaning describing tissue invasion. • Disadvantage: Omits tissue mass deformation and mass-effect dynamics.

• Drawbacks: Fails to incorporate clinical therapy-induced cell death. • Accuracy: Mathematically demonstrated motility coefficients five-fold higher along white-matter tracts than gray matter. • Application: Theoretical basis for most subsequent glioma growth models, including this project's simulation engine. Paper 24: Mathematical Modeling of Glioma Proliferation Harpold et al., 2007 • Methodology: Reviewed clinical translation of biophysical reaction-diffusion and cellular-automata gliomaprogression modelling. • Focus Area: Translational review of mathematical tumour-growth models. • Advantage: Shows how the spatial gradient parameter (ρ/D) can directly guide surgical resection and radiotherapy planning. • Disadvantage: Acknowledges that cellular parameters are highly unidentifiable from standard, sparse MRI scans. • Drawbacks: Routine MRI sequences cannot directly measure underlying cellular densities. • Accuracy: Highlights high spatial variability and modelling error margins during clinical parameter fitting. • Application: Guidance framework for translating mathematical growth models into clinical treatment margins. Paper 25: Computational Model of Hypoxia-Modulated Radiation Resistance Rockne et al., 2015 • Methodology: Integrated cellular growth kinetics with hypoxia-modulated radiation sensitivity parameters derived from pre-treatment ¹⁸F-FMISO-PET scans. • Focus Area: Spatial radiation-resistance zone modelling. • Advantage: Simulates spatial radiation-resistance zones to predict likely recurrence coordinates. • Disadvantage: FMISO-PET imaging is clinically expensive and not widely available. • Drawbacks: High parameter complexity requires specialized computational solvers. • Accuracy: Successfully localized spatial radiation-recurrence boundaries on post-treatment clinical datasets. • Application: Personalized radiotherapy boost-volume planning. Paper 26: Personalized Radiotherapy Design for Glioblastoma Lipková et al., 2019 • Methodology: Unified mathematical tumour-growth simulation, radiomic textures, and genomic mutation data to personalize radiation dose distributions. • Focus Area: Personalized radiotherapy dose optimization. • Advantage: Optimizes radiotherapy volumes to target sub-visual infiltrative cells while sparing healthy tissue. • Disadvantage: Parameter identification requires massive computing clusters and slow numerical pipelines. • Drawbacks: Cannot account for real-time spatial deformation shifts occurring during therapy. • Accuracy: Demonstrated a significant reduction in healthy-tissue radiation dose while retaining target coverage. • Application: Individualized radiotherapy treatment planning. Paper 27: Modeling of Temozolomide Response in Glioma Xenografts Hormuth II et al., 2021 • Methodology: Formulated a spatiotemporal biophysical model simulating tumour proliferation and drug delivery under variable chemotherapy dosing schedules. • Focus Area: Preclinical chemotherapy-response modelling. • Advantage: Preclinically validated against longitudinal animal xenograft drug-delivery and cell-kill data. • Disadvantage: Parameters are derived from animal systems, introducing species-level translation drift. • Drawbacks: Fails to model patient-specific drug-delivery variation across the blood-brain barrier. • Accuracy: Successfully matched xenograft volumetric growth paths across several weeks of treatment.

• Application: Preclinical chemotherapy scheduling optimization. Paper 28: Repurposing the Public BraTS Dataset Sørensen et al., 2024 • Methodology: Developed an 8-step conversion algorithm adapting 3-label preoperative BraTS masks into 2-label postoperative segmentations. • Focus Area: Postoperative segmentation label adaptation. • Advantage: Trains deep-learning models to exclude fluid-filled resection cavities, reducing false positives. • Disadvantage: Segmentation accuracy declines for highly diffuse, non-circumscribed margins. • Drawbacks: Validation remains reliant on a single external cohort. • Accuracy: The 2-label model reached a postoperative median Dice of 0.75 (enhancement) and 0.68 (non-enhancing margins). • Application: Postoperative recurrence-monitoring pipelines. Paper 29: Digital Twins for Predictive Oncology Ghaffarizadeh et al., 2024 • Methodology: Detailed a conceptual framework for bi-directional medical digital twins that update multi-scale models via clinical data assimilation. • Focus Area: Adaptive, patient-specific digital-twin modelling. • Advantage: Supports adaptive, patient-specific treatment optimization as new clinical data arrives. • Disadvantage: Extreme computational overhead is required for running stochastic cell ensembles. • Drawbacks: Framework remains conceptual, with no certified clinical validation to date. • Accuracy: Demonstrated robust mathematical tracking of simulated multi-scale tumour-immune trajectories over time. • Application: Long-term vision for adaptive, personalized oncology platforms. Paper 30: SLIMBRAIN Real-Time Hyperspectral Classification Sancho et al., 2023 • Methodology: Developed the SLIMBRAIN system, capturing hyperspectral imaging and overlaying classification maps onto a LiDAR RGB point cloud. • Focus Area: Real-time intraoperative hyperspectral tissue classification. • Advantage: Maps tumour tissue boundaries in real time during active surgical procedures. • Disadvantage: Depends on specialized, non-standard camera hardware inside the surgical theatre. • Drawbacks: Limited to static visualization, lacking temporal growth modelling. • Accuracy: Processed and classified tissue boundaries at 14 frames per second during active neurosurgical resections. • Application: Real-time intraoperative tissue-boundary guidance.

Project Implementation Framework To translate the findings of the literature survey into a functional tool, a production-grade educational pipeline was implemented. The architecture integrates multi-parametric MRI preprocessing, deep-learning-based segmentation, 3D mesh optimization, biophysical simulation, and an augmented-reality user interface. 1. Data Preparation and Normalization The data-processing module, developed in Python using the SimpleITK library, ingests multi-parametric preoperative volumes (T1, T1Ce, T2, and FLAIR sequences) stored in NIfTI format (.nii.gz). Sequences are aligned using rigid mutualinformation registration with the T1Ce volume as the target coordinate system. Bias-field correction is applied using the N4 algorithm, followed by z-score normalization to standardize voxel intensities across the co-registered brain mask. 2. Deep-Learning Volumetric Segmentation and 3D Mesh Generation A pre-trained 3D nnU-Net model is deployed to identify three tumour subregions: the whole tumour, the tumour core, and the contrast-enhancing tissue. The resulting voxel masks are converted into spatial meshes using the marching-cubes algorithm (VTK library). Raw meshes undergo Laplacian smoothing and quadric decimation, reducing polygon count by up to 85% to target under 50,000 triangles for stable mobile rendering, then export as glTF/GLB files that retain physical coordinates relative to the brain centre. 3. Biophysical Simulation Engine The progression-modelling module simulates spatial tumour growth using a 3D finite-difference solver written in optimized C# within the Unity game engine, based on the reaction-diffusion equation ∂c/∂t = ·(D(x) c) + ρc(1-c/K), where c(x,t) is∇ ∇ spatial tumour-cell density, K is tissue carrying capacity, ρ is the proliferation rate, and D(x) is the diffusion tensor. D(x) is dynamically mapped to gray- and white-matter segments derived from the FLAIR sequence, assigning a five-fold higher motility rate along white-matter pathways. The solver uses a Forward-Time Centered-Space (FTCS) finite-difference scheme with stability bounds Δt ≤ Δx²/(6D_max). The solver supports three interactive, user-controlled clinical scenarios: Surgical Resection (a virtual cutting plane deletes intersecting mesh vertices and recalculates residual tumour volume instantly), Radiotherapy (spatial cell-kill simulated via the linear-quadratic model S(d)=e^-(αd+βd²)), and Chemotherapy (globally scaling down the proliferation parameter ρ to demonstrate slowed tumour progression). 4. Augmented Reality Spatial Presentation Layer The presentation layer is implemented in Unity using AR Foundation to target standard mobile devices. Marker-based registration, centred on a printed QR code, eliminates coordinate drift and anchors the virtual brain model to a stable tabletop coordinate system. The interface offers an interactive timeline for scrubbing through simulated and observed time points, dynamic sectioning planes with custom clipping shaders, opacity sliders to reveal spatial relationships between tumour subregions and surrounding pathways, and dynamic volume calculations that update as the user interacts with the timeline. Architectural Gaps and Future Outlook Preoperative Domain Shift in Postoperative Environments: current segmentation models, typically trained on preoperative scans, struggle when deployed postoperatively, as resection cavities, blood products, and surgical scars are often misclassified as active tumour. Adapting annotation protocols to represent postoperative anatomy, including 2-label formats that separate active enhancing tissue from fluid-filled cavities, is a crucial step toward clinical translation. Downstream Error Propagation: image-processing pipelines are highly vulnerable to cascading error - protocol variation and scanner noise distort raw intensity, registration misalignments introduce spatial coordinate error, boundary errors in deep networks propagate into shape definitions, and voxel variation can distort up to 70% of extracted texture parameters, ultimately producing overfitted and unreliable forecasts. Standardized normalization protocols (e.g., ComBat harmonization) and domain-generalized segmentation networks are required to break this cascade. Visual Over-Certainty and Infiltration: spatial-computing platforms naturally present tumour boundaries as solid, sharply defined 3D meshes, which misrepresents the biological reality of diffuse glioma infiltration extending centimetres beyond visible contrast-enhancing borders. Relying on hard-edged overlays during surgical planning can encourage insufficient resection or radiotherapy margins; clinical visualization interfaces should transition toward probabilistic rendering using volumetric shaders and dynamic opacity to communicate boundary uncertainty directly to the surgeon.

Conclusion Integrating spatial computing, deep-learning-based segmentation, and biophysical modelling offers a powerful approach for visualizing complex intracranial pathologies. This literature survey and implementation report demonstrates that while 3D mesh rendering and multi-parametric segmentation are highly mature, significant translational barriers remain in clinical coordinate registration, scanner standardization, postoperative cavity representation, and individual growth forecasting. For educational and surgical-planning applications, hybrid architectures that combine automated deep-learning pipelines with physics-based modelling and clear uncertainty visualization provide a robust pathway to improve spatial understanding without suggesting false diagnostic certainty.
